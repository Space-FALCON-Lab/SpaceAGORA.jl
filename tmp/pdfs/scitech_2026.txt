A Comparative Analysis of Aerobraking Dynamics Across
Celestial Bodies Using SpaceAGORA.jl
Evan D. Yu∗ , Nihal Simha∗ , and Giusy Falcone†
University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, MI, 48109
Aerobraking has been extensively studied and flight-tested at Mars and Venus, yet comparatively little analysis exists for other targets of interest, most notably Earth-return trajectories
and Saturn’s moon, Titan. This work addresses this gap with a systematic perturbation
analysis of the aerobraking environment performed using SpaceAGORA.jl. SpaceAGORA.jl
is a high-fidelity tool that couples the Global Reference Atmospheric Model Suite with highdegree gravitational harmonics, third-body gravitational effects, and solar-radiation pressure.
Simulator outputs are validated against periapsis and apoapsis altitude histories from Mars
Odyssey and Venus Express, and with propagations from the General Mission Analysis Tool
for Earth-return cases. This work presents a comparative analysis summarizing the relative
influence of each perturbation across the aerobraking environments of Mars, Venus, Earth,
and Titan. Results show that the lunisolar gravitational effects lower Earth periapsis altitudes
by an average of more than 2 km per orbit for high-apoapsis Earth orbit, but are minor for
low-altitude orbits. Titan aerobraking campaigns exhibit the largest variability in terms of
periapsis fluctuations, experiencing drops of more than 60 km in periapsis altitude in one orbit
caused by Saturn’s third-body gravitational perturbations. Due to Titan’s dense atmosphere
and the observed periapsis altitude fluctuations, thermal constraints due to unexpectedly low
periapsis altitudes are a major concern. Finally, a set of dimensionless perturbation parameters
is introduced that provides a framework for quantitatively evaluating the relative contributions
of drag, oblateness, higher-order gravity, and third-body tides along an aerobraking trajectory.

II. Introduction
erobraking is a technique that reduces the orbital energy of a spacecraft through a sequence of controlled
atmospheric passages, potentially decreasing the propellant needs for orbit insertion compared to a fully propulsive
insertion approach. The technique was first demonstrated during a 1991 perigee pass of Japan’s Hiten orbiter [1] and
later matured by NASA’s Magellan experiment at Venus, followed by Mars Global Surveyor, Mars Odyssey, Mars
Reconnaissance Orbiter (MRO), MAVEN, and the European Space Agency’s (ESA) ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter
(TGO) missions [2–5]. ESA’s Venus Express mission further expanded our understanding of aerobraking at Venus [6].
Although these campaigns established core operational principles, each celestial body presents distinct challenges, from
gravity-field harmonics and third-body perturbations to highly variable upper-atmospheric density.
Previous investigations have extensively characterized perturbations at Mars and Venus [2–8]. For other targets,
coverage is patchy; an initial study on Titan aerobraking was conducted for the Titan Saturn System Mission (TSSM), in
particular demonstrating the periodic nature of Saturn’s third-body effects [9]. Earth-return aerobraking studies, on the
other hand, have focused on flight-mechanics constraints [10] or geocentric orbit transfer of small satellites [11], but no
studies have performed a comprehensive analysis of the perturbations affecting a general aerobraking campaign at Earth.
A complicating factor in studying aerobraking is the lack of high-fidelity, fast, and openly-available simulators for
both aeroassist passages and multi-body astrodynamics. Commercial suites such as Ansys STK [12] or a.i. solutions
FreeFlyer [13] often lack high-fidelity atmospheric models and require costly licenses. Government tools such as the
General Mission Analysis Tool (GMAT) [14], Genesis [15], Trick [16], and the Jet Propulsion Laboratory’s (JPL)
Dynamics Algorithms for Real-Time Simulation (DARTS) [17] offer high fidelity but are often export-controlled or lack
aeroassist or aerothermal modeling capabilities. Open-source frameworks give community access, but trade capabilities:
OreKit [18] only provides basic atmospheric models such as the Naval Research Laboratory Mass Spectrometer and
Incoherent Scatter radar Exosphere (NRLMSISE00), Basilisk [19] provides 6-DOF dynamics but, to the authors’
knowledge, does not support analysis of aeroheating metrics, and the original Aerobraking Trajectory Simulator (ABTS)
[20] is Mars-centric and too slow for large Monte Carlo studies.
To address this gap, we introduce SpaceAGORA.jl, an evolution of the Python-based ABTS [20] that natively
incorporates the Global Reference Atmospheric Model (GRAM) Suite that achieves run times an order of magnitude
faster than its predecessor while maintaining high-fidelity gravitational effects and 𝑛-body perturbations. This tool
has been validated against periapsis and apoapsis histories from Mars Odyssey and Venus Express, as well as GMAT
propagation when flight data are unavailable. Using SpaceAGORA.jl, this work presents three key contributions to the
literature: (i) quantifying the impact of major perturbations on aerobraking trajectories at Mars, Venus, Earth and Titan;
(ii) defining high-resolution surveys for Earth-return and Titan insertion trajectories; and (iii) distilling cross-planet

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section III discusses the design and implementation of
SpaceAGORA.jl, including the models and datasets used to determine the forces acting upon the spacecraft, reference
frames, and perturbations. Validation against flight data from Mars Odyssey and Venus Express, as well as against
simulation data from GMAT, is presented in Sec. IV. Section V presents the Monte Carlo dispersions used as well as the
test matrix used for the perturbation analysis. Section VI presents an analysis and discussion of simulated aerobraking
trajectories at Mars, Venus, Earth, and Titan, and Sec. VII provides a comparative analysis of the environment at each
destination and how it affects the trajectory, and introduces the dimensionless perturbation parameters used to quantify
the relative effect of individual perturbations. Section VIII summarizes the analyses, comparison, and conclusions.

III. Simulation Architecture
SpaceAGORA.jl has been developed as a general-purpose 6-DOF trajectory and aerothermal simulation engine,
evolving from the Python-based ABTS [20]. Whereas ABTS was tailored primarily to Mars aerobraking in a point-mass
formulation, SpaceAGORA.jl is designed to support end-to-end analysis from in-orbit propagation through atmospheric
entry, with interfaces and run-time performance intended for autonomy and deep reinforcement learning studies as well
as classical trajectory design. The dynamics engine generalizes the original force model to include third-body gravitation,
solar radiation pressure (SRP), and high-degree gravitational harmonics that capture local mass concentrations for
each central body. Atmospheric environments are modeled using the GRAM Suite, with the option to ingest daily and
historical National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) data for Earth cases, while planetary ephemerides
and orientations are provided by Navigation and Ancillary Information Facility’s (NAIF) SPICE system. SpaceAGORA.jl
also includes engineering-level aerothermal evaluations and 6-DOF attitude dynamics that are validated in separate
work [24]. In this study, we employ the point-mass (3-DOF) configuration of SpaceAGORA.jl to simulate aerobraking
campaigns at Mars, Venus, Earth, and Titan. Validation of this configuration is conducted through comparison with
periapsis and apoapsis altitude histories from previously flown aerobraking missions, as well as with GMAT propagations
when flight data are unavailable.
A. Software Structure
SpaceAGORA.jl is written in Julia, a high-performance scientific computing language, which offers performance
advantages through just-in-time compilation over other high-level languages such as Python and MATLAB while
retaining a clear and expressive syntax that facilitates development and readability. The codebase for SpaceAGORA.jl is
publicly accessible on GitHub at https://github.com/Space-FALCON-Lab/SpaceAGORA.jl. A flowchart of the
architecture of SpaceAGORA.jl is given in Fig. 1. The arrows show the flow of information, and double-headed arrows
indicate two-way information flow. For example, during the propagation of the dynamics, information about the state is
passed into the physical models to calculate forces and torques due to the relevant physical effects. These forces and
torques are then passed back into the propagation.
A key design principle of SpaceAGORA.jl is its modular and extensible architecture. The simulator is organized
around a set of interoperable modules that handle distinct aspects of orbital and atmospheric flight, including trajectory
propagation, atmospheric modeling, gravity field computation, attitude dynamics, and aerothermal assessment. This
modular structure allows users to easily swap out or upgrade components without requiring significant changes to the
overall codebase. For example, one can test different atmospheric models or gravity-field representations by modifying
only a few lines of configuration code.
In addition, celestial bodies can be added or modified with minimal effort through centralized data and configuration
files, which store relevant physical constants, atmospheric parameters, and planetary ephemerides. This framework
enables users to rapidly adapt the tool for mission analysis at new destinations by simply extending the existing
configuration templates. This modularity allows SpaceAGORA.jl to serve both as a flexible, high-fidelity dynamics
engine for mission analysis and as a platform to prototype and test novel guidance and autonomy algorithms.

C. Models
The perturbations considered in SpaceAGORA.jl are 𝑛-body gravitational effects, solar radiation pressure, gravitational harmonics due to non-spherical bodies, and atmospheric density variations. According to Vallado [25], these are
the most significant, non-atmospheric perturbing forces, and others, such as planetary deformation due to solar tides, are
generally negligible.
1. Atmospheric Density
SpaceAGORA.jl employs the GRAM Suite [26], the most accurate atmospheric model currently available for
bodies beyond Earth, to supply nominal and dispersed density profiles for all four targets considered in this study. The
GRAM Suite is used to provide realistic atmospheric conditions, accounting for seasonal density variations, wind, and
thermal effects, as well as spatial atmospheric variations. This is particularly important for modeling the atmosphere
of planets with highly variable atmospheres, such as Venus or Mars. On Venus, due to the extremely slow rotation
rate, the difference in density between the day and night side of the planet can vary up to several orders of magnitude.
This affects aerobraking dynamics in terms of both the thermal and structural loads that the spacecraft will face
[27]. Data from SPICE about the orientation of a celestial body is used to determine the planetocentric latitude and
longitude of the spacecraft for use with GRAM [28], as well as within GRAM itself. On Mars, GRAM provides dust
storm simulation, enabling analysis of the effects of the violent and unpredictable dust storms. GRAM is also more
accurate and generally applicable than exponential or other non-forecast models. SpaceAGORA.jl includes the Whole
Atmosphere Model-Ionosphere Plasmasphere Electrodynamics (WAM-IPE) Forecast/Real Time System (WFS/WRS)
to query historical, real-time, and forecast data for Earth’s atmosphere. Figure 3(a) shows a comparison between
EarthGRAM, NRLMSISE-00 [29], an atmospheric model developed specifically for Earth, and WAM-IPE [30]. Because
the WAM-IPE data does not extend lower than 100km, there is significant error between EarthGRAM/NRLMSISE00
and WAM-IPE where forecast data is unavailable. EarthGRAM and NRLMSISE00, however, match very closely at
these altitudes. In addition, the error of the logarithm base 10 of the density is quite small for altitudes above 100km,
demonstrating agreement in the order of magnitude of the density models compared to WAM-IPE data, as shown in Fig.
3(b). Based on past and planned missions, aerobraking periapsis altitudes at Earth can range from 125km [1] to nearly
200km [11].
2. Gravitational Harmonics

higher-degree and order harmonics can introduce substantial deviations in a spacecraft trajectory when compared to

IV. Validation
SpaceAGORA.jl is validated in the point-mass configuration using the three cases introduced in Sec. III: the Mars
Odyssey and Venus Express aerobraking campaigns, and a GMAT-based Earth-return propagation in the absence of
flight data. Titan is excluded from direct validation because no aerobraking flight data currently exist and GMAT does
not provide a Titan atmospheric model. For all validation cases, solar radiation pressure was found to be negligible over
the aerobraking duration and is therefore omitted from the analysis.

C. Earth
Aerobraking at Earth involves the most complex perturbation environment among the celestial bodies considered in
this study, due to concurrent influences from third-body gravitational effects and Earth gravity harmonics. EarthGRAM
with MERRA2 data is used for atmospheric conditions in this analysis. Figures 17 and 18 show simulation results
employing the initial orbit parameters from Table 6, except with a periapsis altitude set to 250 km and the inclusion of
discrete periapsis-raise maneuvers enforced by a maximum heat-rate constraint. This constraint helps avoid flying in
higher atmospheric density regions where heating constraints may be violated. The maximum heat rate is approximated
as [40]:

High-Apoapsis Earth-Orbit: For high-apoapsis Earth aerobraking trajectories, gravitational perturbations from the
Sun and Moon dominate trajectory evolution, as shown in Figs. 17 and 18(b). The presence or absence of lunisolar
gravitational perturbations creates two distinct trajectory families. When including lunisolar effects, periapsis altitude
decreases substantially, by more than 100 km, necessitating multiple raise maneuvers. The Δ𝑣 required for the cases that
include lunisolar perturbations is 41.78 m/s. Conversely, when these third-body effects are omitted, periapsis altitude
remains stable with minimal decay. This difference in periapsis behavior significantly accelerates apoapsis decay in
trajectories experiencing lunisolar tides, due to greater atmospheric drag at lower periapsis altitudes.
The RAAN behavior (Fig. 18(b)) further highlights the complexity of lunisolar effects. Without lunisolar
perturbations, RAAN remains nearly constant or decrease gradually due to the gravity harmonics of Earth. However,
the introduction of lunisolar perturbations produces distinct mixed-mode oscillations: the Moon gravity generates
short-period RAAN oscillations, while the Sun gravity introduces slower, long-period oscillations. These long-period
oscillations, driven by the slower Earth orbital motion around the Sun, are not immediately obvious during the relative
brief aerobraking campaign time frame. However, this effect becomes increasingly significant over longer timescales.
The combination of these two frequencies results in complex RAAN evolution, clearly distinguishing trajectories
influenced by lunisolar gravitational effects from those affected primarily by Earth gravity harmonics.

Figure 19 illustrates the underlying physical mechanism driving these dynamics. Periapsis altitude variations arise
primarily because the relative gravitational acceleration from the Moon reaches its maximum near the spacecraft
apoapsis. At this distance, the spacecraft experiences a lunar gravitational force that differs markedly in both magnitude
and direction from that acting on Earth, resulting in significant tidal perturbations. These tidal perturbations directly
affect the spacecraft orbital energy, leading to pronounced changes in periapsis altitude. In contrast, when the spacecraft
is near periapsis, the gravitational influence of the Moon is nearly identical for both Earth and spacecraft due to their
proximity, resulting in minimal differential acceleration and leaving the apoapsis altitude largely unaffected.

Titan, the Moon may orbit Earth many times during an aerobraking campaign, and similar trends in the periapsis altitude

where third-body effects are less significant. The third-body effects of the Sun on Mars are negligible compared to the
other perturbations. For both Titan and high-apoapsis Earth-return cases, this sensitivity to third-body phasing implies
that campaign design must account for the timing of atmospheric passes relative to the parent body’s orbit to avoid large,
uncontrolled periapsis variations.
C. Central-Body Gravity
Central-body gravitational harmonics, especially higher-order harmonics beyond the dominant 𝐽2 term, play a
critical role primarily for Mars and, to a lesser extent, for Earth. At Mars, substantial gravitational anomalies driven by
pronounced topographical variations, such as the Tharsis bulge, Hellas Basin, and Olympus Mons, lead to significant
perturbations in spacecraft periapsis altitude and orbital orientation. As illustrated in Fig. 33, Mars exhibits gravitational
anomalies as large as 1000 mGal, significantly exceeding Venus (300 mGal), and Earth (50 mGal). Consequently, for
Mars aerobraking, spacecraft ground-track alignment with these gravitational anomalies can substantially accelerate
apoapsis altitude decay, or conversely, necessitate additional orbit-correction maneuvers if misaligned.
In contrast, Earth’s gravitational field, while significantly varying due to mountain ranges (e.g., Himalayas, Andes),
is partially moderated by large oceanic basins, reducing overall gravitational harmonic perturbations compared to Mars.
For low-altitude Earth trajectories, Earth’s 𝐽2 effect primarily drives orbital precession, with higher-order harmonics
playing a secondary role.

Venus exhibits extremely slow rotation and minimal oblateness, resulting in a very weak 𝐽2 term. Consequently,
gravitational harmonic perturbations are relatively minor, and solar gravitational tides dominate periapsis altitude
dynamics. Titan, tidally locked with Saturn, similarly exhibits minimal gravitational flattening, producing only minor
𝐽2 effects. While higher-order gravitational harmonics are poorly characterized for Titan due to limited gravitational
mapping, any unmodeled harmonics are likely negligible compared to the dominant perturbations from Saturn’s
gravitational influence on aerobraking campaigns.

VIII. Conclusion
Aerobraking provides a propellant-efficient method of reducing spacecraft orbital energy, but its safe execution
depends on accurately capturing how gravitational and atmospheric perturbations reshape the trajectory over hundreds of

27

passages. This work introduced SpaceAGORA.jl, a fast, open-source simulator that couples GRAM-based atmospheric
models with high-degree gravity fields and 𝑛-body dynamics. In its point-mass configuration, the tool was validated
against Mars Odyssey and Venus Express flight data, as well as GMAT-based Earth-return propagations, reproducing
periapsis altitude histories to within roughly 1–3 km and apoapsis altitudes to better than 1%, which is sufficient fidelity
for detailed aerobraking campaign design.
Using SpaceAGORA.jl, a cross-body perturbation analysis for Mars, Venus, Earth, and Titan has been carried
out. The results were interpreted through four dimensionless perturbation parameters, Π3b , Π 𝐽2 , Π 𝐻 , and Π𝐷 , which
compare third-body tides, oblateness, higher-order gravity, and drag to primary gravity. For the geometries studied, these
parameters are typically O (10−4 –10−5 ) in magnitude, so it is their relative ordering that distinguishes regimes. Mars
sits in a gravity-harmonic regime where Π 𝐽2 and Π 𝐻 dominate and kilometer-scale periapsis shifts arise when ground
tracks repeatedly cross major mass anomalies. Venus lies in a solar-tide regime where Π3b ≫ Π 𝐽2 , Π 𝐻 and the Sun
largely controls long-term periapsis evolution. Earth exhibits a clear transition: for high-apoapsis orbits, Π3b initially
exceeds Π 𝐽2 and lunisolar tides drive periapsis decay until the orbit shrinks and the problem becomes 𝐽2 –dominated; for
low-apoapsis orbits, Π 𝐽2 and Π 𝐻 dominate throughout and third-body effects are secondary. Titan combines relatively
large Π3b and Π𝐷 , producing the largest orbit-to-orbit periapsis excursions and the strongest coupling between Saturnian
tides and atmospheric drag among the cases considered.
These trends have direct design implications. At Mars and low-altitude Earth, campaign performance is set mainly
by the choice of periapsis corridor relative to gravity anomalies; at Venus and high-apoapsis Earth, by the epoch and
plane orientation relative to the Sun (and Moon); and at Titan, by the phasing of atmospheric passes within Titan’s orbit
about Saturn. In particular, the combination of strong third-body tides, dense atmosphere, and large natural periapsis
fluctuations at Titan implies that future Titan aerobraking campaigns will require especially conservative thermal
margins and dedicated characterization of the local heating environment. Simulations with a representative spacecraft
geometry yielded peak heating rates of order 0.36 W/cm2 , consistent with staying below heritage limits but highlighting
the need for accurate prediction of orbit-to-orbit heating variations. More broadly, the Π parameters provide a compact
way to classify aerobraking regimes across bodies and orbit geometries, and can be used as a preliminary screening tool
to identify when drag, gravity harmonics, or third-body tides will drive campaign design.
